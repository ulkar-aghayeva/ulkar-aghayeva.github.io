<div><div><div><div><div><div>

  <div><p><strong>Scriabin op. 57 (1907) </strong><a href="https://youtu.be/SwbkirV5k_c?si=wgKGpX3Z3bUYJ-og" rel="noopener"><strong>no. 1. Désir</strong></a><strong> &amp; </strong><a href="https://youtu.be/d7VMLwj-Tkc?si=HeS8DPnW1VAI_Ty9" rel="noopener"><strong>no. 2. Caresse dansée</strong></a>. These two lovely ephemeral pieces both open with an incomplete version of Scriabin’s mystic chord C F♯ B E. The full version of the chord, consisting of an augmented fourth, diminished fourth, augmented fourth, and two perfect fourths, is also known as the Prometheus chord (C F♯ B♭ E A D), used in his op. 60, <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vp386CtJTls" rel="noopener"><strong>Prometheus: The Poem of Fire</strong></a>. </p><p><a href="https://music.apple.com/us/album/yo-yo-ma-plays-bach/175376453" rel="noopener"><strong>Yo-Yo Ma plays Bach</strong></a>, a 1983 album featuring Bach’s arguably less well known Sonatas for viola da gamba/cello and harpsichord. Cello part feels like a knife through warm butter.</p><p><strong>Sarabandes from Bach Cello Suites in </strong><a href="https://youtu.be/6OKG4uCEfVQ?si=8uFGHnuFWJfPtAq8" rel="noopener"><strong>C major</strong></a><strong> and </strong><a href="https://youtu.be/zKCPOwhhbG8?si=ZJwab-BvnUrrikaR" rel="noopener"><strong>E major</strong></a>, played by Jean-Guihen Queyras (overall, his is the <a href="https://youtube.com/playlist?list=OLAK5uy_lgE9Ka74mbgAuQVBqG_2JPMuiwrgJVzEo&amp;si=56NDpSLbhanos-WZ" rel="noopener">best recording</a> of Bach Cello Suites I know of). I made <a href="https://music.apple.com/us/playlist/cello-sarabandes/pl.u-2aoqqAyuNBDBR2E" rel="noopener">a playlist</a> of all Sarabandes from the cello suites, and it’s striking how similar the C major and E major sarabandes are when juxtaposed. </p><p><a href="https://music.apple.com/us/album/rounds/1693868351?i=1693868352" rel="noopener"><strong>Rounds</strong></a><strong> by Jessie Montgomery (2022)</strong>, is an excellent contemporary piece for piano and string orchestra. Very Stravinsky-esque, as my composition teacher says. Yet also unmistakably American. </p><p><a href="https://music.apple.com/us/album/faur%C3%A9-nocturnes/1572081636" rel="noopener"><strong>Fauré piano nocturnes</strong></a>. Especially the last one, no. 13, is, according to my composition teacher, “the best thing ever”. </p><p><a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gOZcwMiZDvo" rel="noopener"><strong>Quando corpus morietur</strong></a>, last duo from Pergolesi’s Stabat Mater. <a href="https://youtu.be/ZILyKGlZVSM?si=OZ5pCcpvDcQukmEN" rel="noopener"><strong>Stabat mater dolorosa</strong></a> is the best known duo from this work but Quando corpus morietur, is, dare I say, even more sublime. Also it feels weighty that Pergolesi wrote this work in the final weeks of his life.</p><p><a href="https://music.apple.com/us/album/faur%C3%A9-complete-cello-works/401486259" rel="noopener"><strong>Fauré cello sonatas</strong></a>. The slow 2nd movements are always the best. </p><p><a href="https://music.apple.com/us/album/faur%C3%A9-piano-quartets/346025798" rel="noopener"><strong>Fauré piano quartets</strong></a>. Likewise, the slow 3rd movements are the best. Collected in <a href="https://music.apple.com/us/playlist/faur%C3%A9-2nd-3rd-movements/pl.u-xlyNNxPCkRgRxvj" rel="noopener">this playlist</a>.</p><p><a href="https://youtu.be/gfUvbHPiulU?si=afs1zrqDVO87zamm" rel="noopener"><strong>Elliott Carter, Caténaires</strong></a> from <a href="https://music.apple.com/us/album/pictures/1037935869" rel="noopener">Two Thoughts about the Piano</a>. Incredible performance by pianist <a href="https://www.conradtao.com/" rel="noopener">Conrad Tao</a>.</p><p><a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ug6Wj8rO-iw" rel="noopener"><strong>Agnus Dei from Bach’s B minor mass</strong></a>. What is there to say. Except that I learned from Christoph Wolff’s lecture at the Bach Virtuosi Festival in May that this Agnus Dei is based on an earlier aria, "Ach, bleibe doch", of his 1735 Ascension Oratorio <em>Lobet Gott in seinen Reichen</em>, BWV 11. Which itself quotes another aria, "Entfernet euch, ihr kalten Herzen", from a lost wedding serenade (1725) (or they are both derived from the serenade).</p><p><a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AkAlRaZO_Zs" rel="noopener"><strong>Piano suite Poetry by The Turtle</strong></a> (<a href="https://www.bso.org/profiles/holden-mui" rel="noopener">Holden Mui</a>). This piece was written in 2021 by then a high school senior (!!!), currently an undergrad in math and music at MIT. I was in awe the first time I listened to it, and loved it even more on repeat re-listens. The style is reminiscent of late Romantic composers, and yet its own thing altogether. Feels very youthful and pure. Also it’s very&nbsp;touching that this is a dedication to the composer’s closest friends. Pieces like this remind me that tonal, harmonious music is far from being explored exhaustively. Still so much to be said in this language.&nbsp;</p></div>
